# Simultaneous Multi-Track PCB Routing – Comprehensive Research Report

## Academic Research (2015–2025) on Multi-Track Routing

**Overview:** Recent EDA research has addressed routing multiple nets (a “bus”) concurrently to meet high-speed design constraints like matched lengths and consistent topologies. The problem gained particular attention after the *2018 CAD Contest at ICCAD* introduced a bus routing challenge[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=Ex%02perimental%20results%20show%20that%20our,the%20nanometer%20era%2C%20the%20scale)[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=However%2C%20not%20much%20work%20addressed,bus%20routing%20problem%20while%20considering). Modern approaches go beyond simple sequential routing; they plan *topology-aware* routes where all traces in a bus follow an **isomorphic topology** (same shape/order) to ease timing closure[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=without%20design%20rule%20violations,M1%29%20Obstacle%20%F0%9D%91%9D3%2C1)[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=Figure%201%3A%20,uniform%20track%20configuration.%20To%20stimulate). Key contributions from 2015–2025 include algorithmic frameworks and contest-winning solutions for simultaneous multi-net routing:

-   **Topology-Matching Bus Routers:** *Hsu et al. (DAC 2019)* presented a *DAG-based obstacle-aware topology-matching bus router*[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=inser%02tion%20to%20meet%20the%20timing,Hardware%20%E2%86%92%20Electronic%20design%20automation). Their algorithm clusters nets by pin topology, then uses a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) search to route all bits of a bus in the specified topology, handling spacing rules and non-uniform track grids[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=are%20summarized%20as%20follows%3A%20%E2%80%A2,routing%20scheme%20that%20alleviates%20routing). A rip-up and re-route scheme resolves congestion. This method outperformed all other teams in the ICCAD’18 contest, with 45–420% lower cost than the next best[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=Ex%02perimental%20results%20show%20that%20our,the%20nanometer%20era%2C%20the%20scale)[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=violations,core%20techniques%20of%20our%20algorithm). *DOI: 10.1145/3316781.3317740.* The authors reported that enforcing identical routing topology (not just equal lengths) for each trace simplifies later buffer insertion and timing calibration[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=without%20design%20rule%20violations,M1%29%20Obstacle%20%F0%9D%91%9D3%2C1)[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=Figure%201%3A%20,uniform%20track%20configuration.%20To%20stimulate).
    
-   **Concurrent Maze Routing (MARCH):** *Chen et al. (DAC 2019)* developed **MARCH** (“Maze Routing under a Concurrent and Hierarchical scheme”) to route all bus traces **concurrently** “like marching in a path”[semanticscholar.org](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/MARCH%3A-MAze-Routing-Under-a-Concurrent-and-Scheme-Chen-Liu/51c06345b3c91962cbcb9dcbac08e8cef393ccb1#:~:text=In%20MARCH%2C%20all%20the%20bits,outperforms%20the%20first%20place). It combines coarse global path planning with fine-grained track assignment for each bit[jingsongchen.github.io](https://jingsongchen.github.io/slides/DAC19-MARCH-slides.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Two%20key%20features%20of,CONSTRUCTION). By routing all bits together, MARCH ensures the bus is matched in topology and was reported to significantly outperform even the contest’s first-place router in some benchmarks[semanticscholar.org](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/MARCH%3A-MAze-Routing-Under-a-Concurrent-and-Scheme-Chen-Liu/51c06345b3c91962cbcb9dcbac08e8cef393ccb1#:~:text=In%20MARCH%2C%20all%20the%20bits,outperforms%20the%20first%20place). *DOI: 10.1145/3316781.3317860.* Its two key features are (1) a **hierarchical** approach – first find a shared path corridor for the bus, then assign individual lines to tracks – and (2) **concurrent routing** of all lines to maintain order and spacing[jingsongchen.github.io](https://jingsongchen.github.io/slides/DAC19-MARCH-slides.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Two%20key%20features%20of,CONSTRUCTION).
    
-   **Obstacle-Avoiding Bus Length-Matching:** *Cheng et al. (IEEE TVLSI 2020)* proposed a bus router that optimizes obstacle avoidance and balanced trace lengths under non-uniform track resources[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=Routing%20under%20a%20Concurrent%20and,1881%E2%80%931892%2C%202020). They focus on single-layer bus routing with length-matching, adjusting routes to avoid obstacles while keeping lengths equal. *Yan (IEEE TCPMT 2022)* tackled **multiple buses** on one layer with *simultaneous escape routing* considerations[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=,of%20electronics%2C%20communications%20and%20computer)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=connections%2C%E2%80%9D%20IEICE%20Transactions%20on%20Fundamentals,Association%20for%20Computing%20Machinery%2C%202019). Yan’s algorithm can route several distinct buses concurrently (considering how one bus might constrain another), and introduced techniques to ensure all nets escape from pin arrays with equal “escape lengths” before entering the bus trunk. *(DOI: 10.1109/TCPMT.2022.3175069)*.
    
-   **Earlier Foundations:** Prior works laid groundwork in bus routing and length matching. For example, *Ozdal & Wong (2006)* formulated length-matched single-layer bus routing for high-speed boards[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=Lakes%20Symposium%20on%20VLSI%2C%20ser,%E2%80%9CA%20fast%20longer%20path%20algorithm). *Kong et al. (DAC 2009)* developed an *Automatic Bus Planner for dense PCBs* that performed global routing and layer assignment for buses, achieving full routing on a complex 7000-net board (this was one of the first dedicated PCB bus routers). *Tseng et al. (IEEE TCAD 2015)* applied an **ILP-based approach** to length-match meander patterns with minimal additional length[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=method%20for%20disordered%20pins%20in,1000%E2%80%931013%2C%202015). These older methods often routed bus traces sequentially or via iterative refinement, whereas newer methods emphasize simultaneous or topology-coupled routing.
    
-   **Multi-Objective Advances:** Research has also addressed related constraints: *Kohira & Takahashi (2010)* introduced the **CAFE router** (Connectivity Aware Frontier Exploration) to route multiple nets on a grid with obstacles, prioritizing connectivity and timing[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=,2380%E2%80%932388%2C%202010). *Chang et al. (ICCAD 2019)* targeted group-based length matching in flip-chip designs, treating bus groups as flows in a negotiable global routing framework[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=,1%E2%80%938). Very recent work (e.g. *Fang et al., DAC 2024*) focuses on **obstacle-aware meandering** for length tuning after initial routing[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=challenges%20on%20automatic%20length%20matching%2C,MSDTW%29%20method%20can)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=In%20the%20aspect%20of%20obstacle,method%20on%20gridded%20space%20and) – ensuring that added serpentine loops fit in available space and even handling differential pairs without uncoupling[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=challenges%20can%20be%20addressed%20through,Experimental). This indicates the academic interest is not only in initial multi-trace routing but also in post-routing adjustments to meet high-speed constraints (skew, impedance, crosstalk).

## Open-Source Implementations & Prototypes

Open-source EDA tools have **limited support** for simultaneous multi-net interactive routing as of 2025. Notable findings:

-   **KiCad:** The popular open-source PCB tool KiCad (v6/v7) does *not* yet support interactive multi-track routing of new traces; it only supports dragging multiple pre-existing tracks together (“multi-drag”). KiCad’s push-and-shove router (PNS) can move a group of parallel tracks while preserving spacing, but there is no feature to *route* a new bus in one go. User discussions confirm this: for example, a KiCad forum thread from 2021 lamented the lack of a plugin or feature for routing 100+ parallel nets – users resorted to manual copy/paste and the push-n-shove router to pack them, noting that the interactive router “cannot ‘force’ squeeze in the 98 remaining tracks… it is wasting space” when trying to insert many parallel traces between two that were manually routed[forum.kicad.info](https://forum.kicad.info/t/plugin-for-routing-parallel-tracks/28831#:~:text=as%20well). This indicates KiCad’s current router doesn’t inherently keep a group of nets together at minimal clearance.
    
-   **KiCad PNS Internal Structure:** Though multi-track *routing* is absent, KiCad’s codebase has relevant pieces. The Push-and-Shove (PNS) router framework already includes a **differential pair router** and a **multi-drag handler**. The code has a class for differential pair routing (`pns_diff_pair_placer`) and a `MULTI_DRAGGER` for moving groups of tracks[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/dir_27bb7c2155040678463315a9cfd15717.html#:~:text=KiCad%20PCB%20EDA%20Suite%3A%20router,cpp). The `MULTI_DRAGGER` structure defines, for each track in the group, an `isPrimaryLine` flag to mark the lead/reference trace, and an `offset` value to maintain relative spacing[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=bool%C2%A0dragOK%20%3D%20false). These suggest a foundation on which simultaneous multi-net routing could be built. Indeed, a KiCad developer in 2023 explored implementing multi-track drag and concluded it *must be done in C++* at the router level (the scripting API cannot capture interactive mouse events)[groups.google.com](https://groups.google.com/a/kicad.org/g/devlist/c/4cXvdDHc3a0/m/vrapAufOBQAJ#:~:text=Hello%20Stuart%2C). He outlined a basic approach: on drag start, identify all selected track-ends, designate the closest as the leader (under the cursor), and move all others in parallel; at a corner, rotate all trace segments 45° together while maintaining spacing[groups.google.com](https://groups.google.com/a/kicad.org/g/devlist/c/4cXvdDHc3a0/m/vrapAufOBQAJ#:~:text=My%20solution%20works%20like%20this,need%20to%20turn%20at%2045deg). This prototype (though for dragging rather than new routing) confirms the viability of moving multiple traces in tandem within PNS.
    
-   **FreeRouting and Autorouters:** The open-source Java autorouter **FreeRouting** can route multiple nets automatically, but it does so through its global routing algorithms, not as an interactive feature. FreeRouting’s interactive mode is essentially single-net push/shove routing with 45° constraints[freerouting.org](https://freerouting.org/freerouting/manual/assist-manual-routing#:~:text=Routing%20,to%20a%20net%2C%20for). It doesn’t provide an interactive simultaneous bus routing mode akin to Altium’s. However, FreeRouting (and advanced open algorithms) do treat multiple nets concurrently during auto-routing, using negotiation-based maze search to distribute routes. Similarly, the experimental **OrthoRoute** plugin (open-source, 2020) uses the PathFinder algorithm (a negotiated A\* search) to autoroute all nets on a Manhattan grid[bbenchoff.github.io](https://bbenchoff.github.io/pages/OrthoRoute.html#:~:text=OrthoRoute%20%E2%80%93%20GPU,as%20a%20KiCad%20plugin). These autorouters illustrate algorithmic techniques (multi-commodity pathfinding, global cost negotiation) that *could* be applied to bus routing, but they are geared toward batch autorouting rather than real-time user-guided routing.
    
-   **Other open projects:** No dedicated open-source “interactive bus router” was identified in repositories. gEDA and other FOSS PCB tools lack such advanced interactive features. Some researchers have released code for contest problems (e.g., bus routing visualizers[github.com](https://github.com/Daikon-Sun/Routing-Visualization#:~:text=,Contest%20on%20Initial%20Detailed%20Routing)[github.com](https://github.com/Daikon-Sun/Routing-Visualization#:~:text=,Track%20Bus%20Routing)), but full algorithm implementations are often not public. There has been interest in the KiCad community for such a feature – as evidenced by KiCad’s developers discussing multi-track routing on forums and the upcoming KiCad 9 roadmap – but at the time of research, no released open-source solution exists. In summary, **the gap between commercial tools and open-source EDA is evident here**: interactive multi-track routing remains a mostly proprietary capability, with KiCad and others just beginning to lay groundwork.
    

## Algorithms & Techniques for Multi-Track Path Planning

Routing multiple parallel traces simultaneously poses unique algorithmic challenges. Researchers and tool developers have employed various strategies:

-   **Grid-Based Maze Search:** A straightforward approach is to extend maze routing (Lee’s algorithm or A\*) to handle multiple nets with spacing constraints. One way is to treat the group of N traces as a single composite object of width = N\*trace + gaps. In a grid model, this can be done by inflating obstacles or running a multi-commodity flow algorithm. For example, the classic *PathFinder* negotiation algorithm (originating in FPGA routing) assigns each net a path, then iteratively resolves conflicts[bbenchoff.github.io](https://bbenchoff.github.io/pages/OrthoRoute.html#:~:text=OrthoRoute%20%E2%80%93%20GPU,as%20a%20KiCad%20plugin). This effectively routes nets “simultaneously” by allowing temporary overlaps and then spreading them out. Academic algorithms like CAFE (which expands a wavefront for multiple nets together on a grid) also fall in this category[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=,2380%E2%80%932388%2C%202010). However, pure grid-based approaches can be slow for interactive use, and they don’t easily support arbitrary-angle routing or push/shove.
    
-   **Coupled Path Planning:** Some techniques explicitly plan a **common route** for the bus as a whole, then place individual traces along that route. This is seen in both research and commercial “sketch routing.” The idea is to first find a collision-free *corridor* from source to destination that can accommodate the entire bundle. Then, each trace is assigned a track within that corridor (preserving order). The DAG-based method by Hsu et al. (2019) essentially did this: they reduced the search space by clustering and then connecting pin groups via a single bus path[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=are%20summarized%20as%20follows%3A%20%E2%80%A2,routing%20scheme%20that%20alleviates%20routing). Similarly, eCADSTAR’s semi-auto *Bus Route* (discussed below) uses a sketched guide path and then fits all nets into it[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=Any%20combination%20of%20single,are%20split%20to%20avoid%20obstacles)[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=The%20width%20of%20the%20routing,in%20the%20Bus%20Route%20dialog). This two-phase approach (coarse path + fine assignment) ensures spacing compliance by design. It’s algorithmically akin to planning for a “thick wire” then splitting it. The trade-off is that it may require backtracking if the chosen corridor later proves too narrow (obstacles forcing one trace out).
    
-   **Real-Time Iterative Routing:** In interactive scenarios, a **leader-follower** heuristic is common. The user’s cursor effectively steers a *leader* trace, and the algorithm computes positions for followers at each step. The followers can either mirror the leader’s incremental path (offset by fixed distances), or slightly lag behind to avoid obstacles. A pseudo-code outline of such an algorithm:
    
    pseudo
    
    Copy code
    
    `onMouseMove(cursor):     desiredSegment = compute_new_segment_from_leader(cursor_position)     for each trace i in bus group:         offset_vec = offset_distance[i] in perpendicular direction         new_segment[i] = parallel to leader desiredSegment, shifted by offset_vec     if any new_segment collides with obstacle:         if pushEnabled: attempt push obstacle(s) away for that segment         if push fails or not enabled: mark routing blocked (cannot continue straight)     place all new_segments if clear`
    
    At a corner, this means the leader turns (e.g., 45°), and each follower must turn such that spacing is maintained – often the inner traces will have a shorter turn radius than outer ones. Interactive routers handle this by introducing a slight phase difference: the outer traces continue forward a bit before turning so that all end up aligned at the new angle[groups.google.com](https://groups.google.com/a/kicad.org/g/devlist/c/4cXvdDHc3a0/m/vrapAufOBQAJ#:~:text=selected%2C%20then%20deselect%20everything%20that,need%20to%20turn%20at%2045deg). This ensures no spacing violations on the inside of the bend. Maintaining fixed clearance in real-time is essentially a constraint satisfaction problem solved by simple geometry in this case (computing the correct stagger for corners).
    
-   **Obstacle Avoidance Strategies:** For N traces, obstacles can affect some lines and not others. Algorithms differ on how to handle this:
    
    -   A conservative approach is **treating any obstacle that blocks one line as blocking the whole group**, forcing the user (or autorouter) to detour the entire bus. This keeps the bus tightly grouped but can lead to longer routes.
        
    -   A more flexible approach is **partial detours**: if one or two traces of the group encounter an obstacle (e.g., a via or pad), those traces could snake around it within the clearance margin while the rest of the bus goes straight. After the obstacle, the straggling trace rejoins the bundle. This is complex to do automatically in real-time (it effectively requires splitting and re-merging the routing operation), but some autorouters do this in a post-processing step[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=to%20sketch%20an%20approximate%20path,are%20split%20to%20avoid%20obstacles)[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=resolved%20as%20routes%2C%20if%20possible%2C,are%20split%20to%20avoid%20obstacles). Interactive tools typically opt for simplicity: either push the obstacle (if movable) or stop the bus and let the user choose a new path.
        
    -   **Dynamic spacing adjustment:** Another tactic around obstacles is to momentarily reduce the gap between traces to squeeze through a tight spot. Commercial tools allow this on-the-fly: e.g., in Altium the user can press `C` to converge all traces to minimum spacing before entering a choke point[altium.com](https://www.altium.com/documentation/altium-designer/pcb/routing/interactive-multi?srsltid=AfmBOopDKMQd55Qwk0Fz4BWJIo1Fyi_V1NhDybhb3qh-us4Q8uhGLaXT#:~:text=routing,modes%2C%20switch%20routing%20layers%2C%20etc). Algorithms supporting this would temporarily override the clearance rules (down to the allowed minimum) and then expand the spacing back after the obstacle. Implementation-wise, this is straightforward: the “offset” between traces is not fixed but can be shrunk to the min clearance if needed. However, doing it automatically must be carefully controlled to avoid signal integrity issues (you wouldn’t want long stretches of minimum spacing).
        
-   **Optimization vs. Interactivity:** There is a clear *trade-off between optimal solutions and interactive performance*. Techniques like integer programming or evolutionary algorithms (e.g., one paper used **Differential Evolution** to find optimal bus routes on a graph[ccp.caf.ufv.br](https://ccp.caf.ufv.br/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/POCVitor_Hugo.pdf#:~:text=process%20can%20be%20separated%20in,IC%2C%20routing%2C%20bus%2C%20differential%20evolution)[ccp.caf.ufv.br](https://ccp.caf.ufv.br/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/POCVitor_Hugo.pdf#:~:text=global%20routing%2C%20we%20use%20a,the%20Very%20Large%20Scale%20Integrated)) can yield very high-quality routes but are too slow for a designer-in-the-loop scenario. In contrast, interactive routing favors greedy, fast heuristics (like the leader-follower approach above or simple real-time A\* searches) that may not always find the absolutely shortest or most balanced path, but respond within milliseconds to user input. A\* itself can be used for interactive routing of a single trace; extending it to N traces could mean running A\* N times (one per net) with added constraints to keep them together, or running one search in an expanded state space. The latter becomes exponential if done naively. Therefore, practical interactive routers use incremental methods or simplified models (e.g., treat the bus as one wide trace for pathfinding).
    

In summary, multiple algorithmic paradigms exist: **(1)** unified path search for the bus (ensures coherence but can be complex), **(2)** independent path search per net with periodic synchronization to maintain order/spacing (simpler, but nets might diverge if not careful), and **(3)** physics-inspired “rubber band” models where traces are connected by springs at fixed distances and you drag them (the push-and-shove engine can be viewed in this light, treating clearances as forces). The best solution often combines these – e.g., plan a coarse route then use a push-and-shove engine to refine it in real-time.

## Related Routing Problems and Solutions

Simultaneous multi-track routing is related to several other PCB routing capabilities and constraints. Understanding these helps place multi-track routing in context:

-   **Differential Pair Routing:** This is essentially a special case of multi-track routing with N=2. Differential pair routing engines maintain a fixed gap between two traces and typically enforce phase alignment (the two traces must remain close together except for intentional uncoupling). Many techniques used for diff pairs extend to multi-trace buses. For instance, KiCad’s diff-pair router treats the pair as a unit, routing two lines at once with a gap set by design rules[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/6.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html#:~:text=The%20differential%20pair%20router%20will,a%20different%20distance%20apart%20from). It automatically inserts coupled vias (dropping two vias side by side) when changing layers[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/6.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html#:~:text=minimize%20the%20length%20of%20track,differential%20pair%20is%20not%20coupled) and tries to minimize any uncoupled sections[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/6.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html#:~:text=The%20differential%20pair%20router%20will,a%20different%20distance%20apart%20from). This mirrors what a bus router must do for more lines. One difference is that diff pair algorithms often dynamically adjust one trace’s path to keep length balanced with the other (for signal timing within the pair), whereas for a bus, timing is balanced across the whole group (which may require meanders on some lines after routing). Nonetheless, the *leader-follower concept* in diff pair routing (often one trace is treated as the primary, and the second is computed as an offset copy) is directly applicable to a bus – just with multiple followers instead of one. Many codebases (like KiCad’s PNS) explicitly separate diff pair handling, which suggests implementing multi-track could leverage similar structures (e.g., iterating the logic for N-2 more lines).
    
-   **Bus Escape Routing (Pin Escapes):** Routing out of a fine-pitch component (like a BGA or a connector with many pins in a row) is a precursor to bus routing. Escape routing deals with the “fanout” from pins: you often route differential pairs or bus bits out from a dense pin field to a more open area, then those traces form a bus. Specialized algorithms exist for **ordered escape routing** – ensuring that when traces exit a BGA, they do so in an order that matches how you want to form the bus on the board. Research by Lee et al. (EDAPS 2013) looked at escape routing for differential pairs[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=of%20Integrated%20Circuits%20and%20Systems%2C,2565%E2%80%932571%2C%202015), and more recent work by Wu et al. (2013) and Yan (2022) addressed escape routing for buses with mismatched pin ordering[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=,based)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=Circuits%20and%20Systems%2C%20vol,2971%E2%80%932978%2C%202009). The gist is that sometimes the “natural” order of pins is not the order you want the traces in once they form a parallel run. Some tools (like eCADSTAR’s auto reordering option[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=Auto%20minimize%20crossed%20connection)) can swap the assignment of traces to pins within the escape zone to minimize crossings. This is a complex problem (related to the NP-hard “minimum wire crossing” problem), and solutions involve either brute-force searching small permutations or formulating it as a graph problem. In implementation, *bus routing features often rely on the user to label nets in sequence* or use net classes that imply order, to avoid having to do automatic reordering. In KiCad, for example, one might name nets with suffixes 0,1,2… and the expectation is they’ll be routed in that numerical order. A robust multi-route implementation might incorporate a simple ordering heuristic or at least warn the user if net order will cause crossings.
    
-   **Length Matching and Timing Constraints:** High-speed buses (like DDR address lines) require that all signals have matched or within-window lengths. Multi-track routing inherently makes it easier to length-match because you route all lines together over the main distance – any differences will come from initial fanouts or slight detours. Many bus routers in literature explicitly include a phase for length equalization. For example, Ozdal’s 2006 algorithm inserted “accordion” sections to satisfy max/min length constraints[baloneymath.github.io](https://baloneymath.github.io/files/DAC19_bus.pdf#:~:text=frameworks%20considering%20the%20length,and%20N5%20being%20deployed%20in). Tseng’s 2015 ILP approach tried to minimize additional length while meeting timing by adjusting meander placements[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=method%20for%20disordered%20pins%20in,4%2C%20pp). In practice, commercial tools often integrate a **length tuning tool** that works on an existing routed bus: you select the bus and invoke “tune length” and the tool adds serpentine patterns to shorter traces until all are matched. Some interactive routers allow you to constrain length during routing – for instance, by showing a meter or by dragging one trace of the bus a bit extra to add length – but typically length tuning is a second step after the bus is routed. A related aspect is **skew (delay) tuning**, which might involve delay serpentine or “accordion” structures. Multi-track routing lays the foundation (all traces take the same general path, so their base lengths are similar), and then automated tuning can fine-tune each net. In KiCad’s PNS, length tuning is supported for single nets and diff pairs; extending it to a group is conceptually straightforward (just iterate over the group).
    
-   **Topology Matching:** We’ve discussed this in academic context – ensuring all bits of a bus share the same route shape. This matters not just for pure timing, but also for signal integrity and noise coupling. If all traces run together, they experience similar noise environments. If one trace took a wildly different path, it could have different coupling to aggressors or grounds. Topology matching is essentially guaranteed if you do interactive multi-routing (since you are literally dragging them together). However, one nuance is **T-branch or star topologies** (often in memory or RF routes). Sometimes a “bus” might have split destinations (one net branches to two chips). Interactive multi-routing doesn’t directly handle branching (that typically requires placing a via or a testpoint where the branch occurs, then routing two buses). Topology-aware autorouters (like Chang’s ICCAD’19 work[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19195v1#:~:text=matching%20bus%20routing%20considering%20nonuniform,for%20isolated%20word%20recognition%2C%E2%80%9D%20IEEE)) can enforce that branching structure. In a manual context, the designer would handle the branch by routing one leg of the branch at a time or duplicating the route for each branch. So while topology matching in the sense of shape is covered by multi-routing, handling branches is outside its scope – but it’s worth noting if implementing in KiCad: the tool should perhaps disable multi-routing when a net branch (a node with more than 2 pins) is involved, or only allow it for point-to-point connections.
    
-   **Crosstalk and Spacing Rules:** Multi-track routing inherently means traces run parallel for a long distance, raising the possibility of crosstalk. High-speed design rules often specify not just min spacing, but sometimes **staggered spacing** or shielding for parallel runs. For instance, one might route 8 signals and then a ground guard trace, then another 8 signals, etc., to break up crosstalk groups. Some advanced bus routing features allow inserting shield traces (eCADSTAR can automatically insert a ground net as a shield line at set intervals in a bus group[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=Differential%20Pair)[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=Shield%20signal)). Implementation-wise, that means the router treats the shield like another net in the group, but one that doesn’t need length matching. For now, a basic multi-route likely wouldn’t handle that – it would assume the user has already decided the grouping (like including ground nets in the selection if they want them routed together). As long as spacing rules are respected, crosstalk is “managed” but not actively minimized by basic multi-routing (except through manual spacing adjustments by the user).
    

In short, multi-track routing ties into differential pair handling (N=2 case), bus escape ordering, length tuning, and electromagnetic concerns. A fully-featured solution will consider these, but an MVP implementation can focus on *geometry (spacing and parallelism)*, leaving length and SI optimization to subsequent steps or user control.

## Technical Challenges in Interactive Multi-Trace Routing

Implementing simultaneous multi-net routing (especially in an interactive PCB editor) presents several technical challenges:

-   **Real-Time Performance:** The router must maintain interactive speeds (typically updating the routing within a few **tens of milliseconds** per user action, to feel fluid). This means the algorithm for moving multiple traces must be highly optimized. Computing clearance for one trace is already complex (push & shove involves searching nearby geometry and possibly rerouting other traces). Doing this for N traces together multiplies the work. A naive approach might handle each trace serially, which could bog down. Effective strategies might use a combined obstacle map for the whole group or reuse distance computations. As an example, KiCad’s developer noted the importance of efficient geometry and “force propagation” in their push-shove implementation[archive.fosdem.org](https://archive.fosdem.org/2015/schedule/event/pcb_routing/#:~:text=Interactive%20%28a,tuning%20and%20differential%20pair%20routing) – essentially treating obstacles with an octagonal clearance and pushing them out. Extending that to N traces might involve treating the outer boundary of the N-trace bundle as the “shape” to push obstacles with, which could actually reduce computations (one larger shape instead of N small ones). Another performance trick is to limit how far ahead the algorithm looks – interactive routers often only look at local obstacle interactions rather than plan the entire path (since the user is guiding it anyway). Overall, ensuring the multi-route doesn’t lag or stutter is crucial for usability.
    
-   **Maintaining Spacing Constraints:** The core requirement is that a fixed clearance is maintained between every pair of adjacent traces in the group at all times. This is straightforward on straight segments (just lay them out with the clearance gap). The **difficult part is corners and arbitrary angles**. When turning, the inner traces tend to come closer. The router must either widen the separation during the turn or introduce stagger so that inner traces turn earlier than outer ones. This geometric adjustment can be tricky to compute on the fly. A partial solution is to use filleted corners (curves) or “arcified” corners so that the inner and outer trace paths are concentric arcs – but most PCB tools stick to 45° miters, not arbitrary arcs, for simplicity. Thus, the implementation might pre-compute an offset pattern for a 45° turn: e.g., if trace separation is *d*, then in a 45° bend the traces need to be staggered by *d* along the corner. If not, you end up with the inner corner spacing < *d*. Some tools might momentarily violate spacing during the drag (with a warning) and fix it when the route is completed by adjusting segment lengths. But ideally, the router enforces it live. This is a very geometric challenge – essentially a problem of offset curves. Libraries for computing offset paths (like computing parallel curves) can help here.
    
-   **Obstacle Avoidance and Interaction:** When pushing obstacles (other tracks, vias, components), the complexity grows with N. Consider pushing another trace that’s in the way: if a single new track pushes an old track, that’s fine. But if an entire bus is pushing, we might need to push a *swath* of existing traces to create a channel. If the router only pushes one obstacle at a time, the presence of N closely spaced new traces could mean it has to push the same obstacle N times (once for each new trace as it approaches). A clever approach is to *inflate* obstacles by the bus width so that if any one trace would collide, you treat it as the whole bus colliding at once – then push the obstacle once by the required distance (which would be the clearance from the outermost trace). Essentially, rather than push one track aside by 0.2mm for each of 8 traces sequentially (total 1.6mm), push it once by 1.6mm when the bus as a whole encounters it. This requires calculating the “footprint” of the bus – which is dynamic, especially if the bus is not strictly linear at that moment. The PNS router’s existing shove algorithm would need extension to consider group moves. This is doable but intricate. Additionally, *via pushing* for a bus (especially if changing layers) must ensure that when dropping a set of vias, they all fit. For example, Altium when switching layers will insert a “via shield” pattern – the bus’s vias are spaced by the bus clearance. If an obstacle (like another via) needs to be shoved, it must be shoved enough to clear all of them. So the algorithm must compute the worst-case interference and resolve that.
    
-   **Partial Failure Handling:** Inevitably, there will be scenarios where not all traces can continue forward – e.g., the top trace of the group hits an obstacle that can’t be moved (say a fixed component), but lower traces have room. What should the interactive router do? Some possibilities:
    
    -   **Stop the entire group** at that point, effectively saying “route blocked” (the user then might back up or try a different path).
        
    -   **Drop the blocked trace** out of the group: i.e., let the other traces continue, and leave the stuck one at the obstacle (to be routed later or via a different layer). This is tricky to present in UI and could confuse the designer (“why did one net stop routing?”), but it’s exactly what a semi-automatic router like eCADSTAR can do in completion stage: any incompletes are left behind[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=When%20you%20complete%20the%20sketched,then%20they%20are%20left%20unrouted).
        
    -   **Bypass the obstacle for that trace** and rejoin: essentially, temporarily detour just that one trace around (above or below) the obstacle and merge it back with the group. This keeps the group moving but introduces a length difference and possibly a via. It’s advanced to automate; likely beyond an initial implementation. The user can always manually do this: they could route that one trace separately for a moment and later align it back.
        
    
    For a first implementation, the safest behavior is to **stop when any trace is blocked** (with maybe a message or just the usual routing block indicator). The designer can then choose to push harder (if push-and-shove mode is on, maybe try a different angle or move things manually) or reroute around. In push modes, ideally the tool will try everything to avoid total failure (including pushing multiple obstacles, hugging closer, etc., as long as design rules allow). Altium’s router, for instance, will indicate if it *cannot* continue the route in push/hug mode by showing a blockage marker[altium.com](https://www.altium.com/documentation/altium-designer/pcb/routing/interactive-multi?srsltid=AfmBOopDKMQd55Qwk0Fz4BWJIo1Fyi_V1NhDybhb3qh-us4Q8uhGLaXT#:~:text=,the%20Interactive%20Router%20hug%20existing). A similar approach in KiCad would be reasonable.
    
-   **Integration with Existing Push-and-Shove and Undo:** Multi-route must integrate with KiCad’s **push/shove engine**. PNS is designed around shoving one item at a time; extending it to multiple might involve invoking shove moves for each new segment of each net. We must also ensure the operation is **atomic for undo** – if the user routes a bus through a complex path and then hits Undo, it should remove all segments of all nets that were added, not only one. This likely means the routing tool needs to manage a unified undo transaction grouping all trace insertions. KiCad’s tool framework supports grouping actions, but it’s a detail to implement.
    
-   **User Interface and Experience:** From a UX standpoint, challenges include selecting the nets (KiCad currently can select multiple items, so that’s fine), initiating the command, and providing feedback. For example, KiCad would need a shortcut or button for “Route Multiple Nets” similar to its “Route Diff Pair”. The UI should probably highlight the group being routed (maybe draw a temporary outline around the bundle). Also, showing some form of **ghost/preview** of the bundle as you move the cursor is helpful (Altium does this – you see faint lines for where the traces will go as you move). This preview needs to update in real-time with spacing and angles. KiCad’s current router does preview a single trace segment under the cursor; extending that to N previews is feasible (just draw more lines). Another UI consideration is how to terminate the route: presumably, as with single nets, a click or double-click will end routing for all nets. If some nets reach their target pad before others (maybe the target connectors are staggered), the router could either terminate each net individually as it reaches the end or keep them “dangling”. Altium’s documentation implies you route until all nets reach a destination pad or via – you can also terminate by clicking and it will finish the segments to the current point[portal.ecadstar.com](https://portal.ecadstar.com/contents/services/ecs_doc/2025.0/OnlineHelp/en/Content/PCB/command_help/com_semiauto_busroute.html?TocPath=eCADSTAR%20PCB%20Editor%7CProcess%20Flow%20for%20PCB%20Design%20%7CRouting%20and%20Planes%7CManual%20and%20Interactive%20Routing%7C_____17#:~:text=specified%20in%20the%20Bus%20Route,dialog). It’s important for the implementation to handle the case where nets have different endpoints (e.g., an 8-bit bus where each trace ends at a different pin, possibly not aligned). Typically, you route the longest common portion together, then finish individual traces one by one. The tool might automatically break the group when necessary.
    
-   **Testing and Edge Cases:** A challenge in development will be handling corner cases like: extremely tight U-turns (the bus doubling back on itself – likely not allowed because spacing would fail), routing the bus into a dead-end then backing out (undoing gracefully), interactions with other interactive features (what if the user tries to tune length on one of the nets mid-route? Likely disallowed during routing mode). Also, things like nets of different widths in the group – presumably the group should have homogeneous trace widths, but if not, the clearance might need to be adjusted pairwise. Commercial tools usually require the selected nets to use the same clearance rules or at least handle the largest clearance among them. KiCad’s net classes could enforce uniform width/gap for a bus net class.
    

Overall, these technical challenges are surmountable with careful design. Many have been solved in closed-source tools, but reimplementing them requires attention to computational geometry and robust handling of many simultaneous constraints. Performance tuning (perhaps caching obstacle searches, using spatial indices, etc.) will be key to make the multi-trace router *feel* as smooth as single-trace routing.

## Implementing Multi-Track Routing in KiCad’s PNS Router

Finally, we turn to concrete steps and suggestions for implementing simultaneous multi-net routing in KiCad’s push-and-shove (PNS) router framework. The goal is to **extend existing infrastructure, not rewrite from scratch**, leveraging KiCad’s proven PNS algorithms for single nets, dragging, and diff pairs.

**1\. Architectural Extension:** KiCad’s router is structured around the `pns_router` class and various “placer” helpers (for single lines, diff pairs, etc.). The cleanest approach is to introduce a new *placer* class, e.g. `PNS::BUS_PLACER` (or extend `DIFF_PAIR_PLACER` to general N). This class would handle the group routing logic, analogous to how `DIFF_PAIR_PLACER` manages two coordinated `LINE_PLACER` instances for the P and N nets. Internally, we can model a bus as a collection of `PNS::LINE` objects (one per net) plus some shared state (like desired spacing). We’d likely create something akin to a `MULTI_LINE_PLACER` that contains multiple `LINE_PLACER` instances or at least multiple target endpoints, and orchestrates them.

-   *Selecting Nets:* The router tool should allow selecting multiple nets’ start points. KiCad already supports multi-select of pads or tracks, so the user would select the pads of the nets they want to route (as Altium does)[altium.com](https://www.altium.com/documentation/altium-designer/pcb/routing/interactive-multi?srsltid=AfmBOopDKMQd55Qwk0Fz4BWJIo1Fyi_V1NhDybhb3qh-us4Q8uhGLaXT#:~:text=Before%20choosing%C2%A0the%20command%2C%C2%A0select%20the%20source,child%20pads%20in%20a%20component). The new command (e.g., “Route Bus”) would gather those nets, sort them in some order (perhaps left-to-right relative to the direction of routing or as selected), and initialize the bus placer with those net IDs.
    
-   *Leader and Followers:* Designate one net as the **leader** (likely the one closest to the cursor when starting, or simply the first in selection). This will be analogous to `leaderItem` in the multi-drag structure[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=%E2%97%86%20leaderItem). The other nets are followers. In the bus placer, whenever we compute a new segment, we’ll compute it for the leader using the normal interactive routing (which uses `pns_line_placer` + PNS shove/hug logic to propose a segment toward the cursor or target). Then we calculate follower segments: essentially copy the leader’s segment vector, but shifted perpendicular to its direction by the required offset for each net. The offset values can be pre-computed from the net ordering and clearance rule (for instance, +d, +2d, ... for nets on one side of leader and -d, -2d, ... for the other side if the leader is a center net, or 0, d, 2d... if leader is one edge). A simple strategy is to pick one edge net as leader so that all offsets are on one side.
    
-   *Use of `pns_multi_dragger` concepts:* The multi-drag code already handles maintaining relative offsets of a group of *existing* tracks during drag. We can mirror some of that logic. Notably, `MULTI_DRAGGER::MDRAG_LINE` stores `isPrimaryLine` and `offset` for each line in the group[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=bool%C2%A0dragOK%20%3D%20false). We can use a similar structure for routing: each net in the bus group has an offset (in world units, perhaps) and a flag for primary. When the leader moves, instead of dragging existing segments, we are creating new ones, but conceptually it’s similar – we want the followers to “move” in parallel at a fixed offset. If feasible, one could even instantiate a `MULTI_DRAGGER` in a special mode to handle the simultaneous movement, but since that’s designed for dragging already-laid tracks, it might not directly apply to laying new tracks. Instead, borrow the math/logic: how it calculates new positions for each line given a leader’s motion. Specifically, functions like `multidragWalkaround()` and `multidragShove()` in PNS likely compute how far each follower can go without violation[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=Referenced%20by%20PNS%3A%3AMULTI_DRAGGER%3A%3AmultidragShove,11)[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=%E2%97%86%20isPrimaryLine). That logic could inform how we extend segments.
    
-   *Spacing Enforcement:* The `offset` stored is the ideal spacing in board units (e.g., 0.25 mm or whatever). To ensure spacing, we must integrate that into clearance checking. One approach is to temporarily create a **clearance rule override** for the nets in the group such that their clearance to each other is ignored (because we position them explicitly) but their clearance to everything else accounts for the group. Or simpler: when testing collisions for a follower’s new segment, ignore collisions with its fellow group members’ new segments (since we deliberately overlap them in initial computation and will adjust them). Instead, enforce their spacing by construction: the offset placement ensures they are exactly at clearance distance. We should double-check using KiCad’s clearance engine that this yields no DRC violations – it should not, if placed correctly.
    
-   *Obstacle Pushing:* We rely on PNS’s shove engine for obstacles. We will call the shove logic for each new segment. However, to avoid redundant pushes, we can optimize: if the leader segment triggers a push of an obstacle, that obstacle likely needs to move a bit extra to clear not just the leader but also the followers behind it. Perhaps we can compute the total envelope of the bus segment and use that for collision detection. Concretely, if the bus is moving eastward and turning slightly, we can take the rectangle (or polyline) encompassing all N new segments and feed that to the collision checker. This is complex to implement from scratch. Alternatively, perform collision checks for each follower segment in turn: when the leader segment is placed, then attempt to place follower1 – if it hits something, push it; then follower2, etc. If an obstacle (say a via) gets pushed by follower1, by the time we check follower2 it might already be clear. If not, push again. This sequential push is easier and probably acceptable for moderate N (the user typically routes maybe 4–16 nets at once, not 1000).
    
-   *Corner adjustments:* When the leader changes direction (e.g., a 45° turn), the followers will automatically form the same angle but their vertex point might need trimming or extending. For example, the inner follower might overshoot the leader’s corner if done naively. One could detect a corner in the leader’s path and then shorten the previous segment of inner nets or lengthen the new segment to maintain distance. The multi-drag code has flags like `cornerIsLast` and distances like `cornerDistance` and `leaderSegDistance` that appear related to handling corners[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=%E2%97%86%20cornerDistance)[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=%E2%97%86%20leaderSegDistance). Studying that code could provide a recipe for how to stagger. In diff pair implementation, typically the router will ensure the turning point of both traces is such that both traces remain ~parallel and separated by the gap through the turn. Extending to N, you might not achieve perfect parallelism in a single 45° mitered turn, but as long as you ensure minimum gap, it’s acceptable. We might implement a simple rule: when a turn is made, space out the entry point of each net by `offset` along the route. This could be done by inserting a tiny straight segment before the turn for outer traces so that they start turning slightly later than inner ones. This level of nuance can be iterative – initial version might skip it, and rely on the fact that a 45° bus where traces are offset orthogonally should inherently have a bit of extra spacing on inner side (since the inner trace’s path is shorter). A quick geometry calc can confirm needed stagger.
    
-   **Integration with PNS Flow:** KiCad’s `pns_router` likely has a state machine for routing vs dragging vs diff pair routing. We’ll add a new state for multi-net routing. When active, mouse movements and clicks are handled by our bus placer instead of the single-net placer. The bus placer calls underlying functions to add segments to each net’s routing. If push-and-shove is on, it will call the shove routines. If walkaround is on, it calls walkaround for each or overall. We should reuse as much as possible: for instance, call the existing `Route()` or `TryRouteSegment()` function for each segment, but perhaps with a flag to ignore pushing group mates.
    
-   **Pseudo-code example (conceptual):**

-   **Via Insertion and Layer Changes:** When the user hits the layer-change hotkey (typically `V` in KiCad) during a bus route, the router should place a *via pair* (or via array). Differential pair code provides a good template: KiCad’s diff pair router drops two vias in one go, with the correct spacing[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/6.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html#:~:text=minimize%20the%20length%20of%20track,differential%20pair%20is%20not%20coupled). For a bus, if changing from layer1 to layer2, we need to insert one via per net, arranged in a pattern. The simplest pattern is to maintain the relative positions: e.g., if nets are running horizontally spaced, dropping vias will produce a similar pattern of offsets. We must ensure the via clearance rules – effectively the vias should form a mirror of the trace pattern on the via layer. This could be done by offsetting each via by the same offsets as the tracks. If any via would collide (e.g., with a thru-hole pad or another via), push-and-shove can relocate it slightly (KiCad’s shove can move vias too). We also have to connect the vias to the new layer’s routing segments. This is mostly handled by KiCad’s existing via placement logic, but extended to multiple items. A possible implementation is to call the via placement for the leader, then for each follower net, call via placement with an X/Y offset relative to leader’s via. If an obstacle prevents placing a via at one of those offset locations, perhaps the bus via drop fails as a whole (or we push the obstacle, or as a last resort drop those vias that can be placed and leave one net un-via’d – but that complicates things; better to require space for all or none).
    
-   **Undo Considerations:** Each addition of segments (and vias) for all nets should be grouped in a single undo action. KiCad’s action manager allows grouping multiple low-level actions (like “add segment to net1”, “add segment to net2”, etc.) into one user action. The bus placer should leverage that: start a group at the beginning of a routing step, commit all segments, then end group. This way, one undo removes the whole step for all nets.
    
-   **Reusing Diff Pair Code:** It’s worth examining how `pns_diff_pair_placer` is implemented. It likely creates two `LINE_PLACER` instances and tries to keep them in sync. Perhaps it even uses a combined clearance check. By reading that code (which is available in KiCad’s source), we can mirror its structure for N>2. Diff pair code might handle the corner staggering for two traces – generalizing that logic to N is easier once understood for N=2.
    
-   **Testing Approach:** Initially, implement with a small N (like 3 or 4) to verify behavior, then scale up. Test in various modes (shove, walkaround, etc.). Use simple scenarios (straight bus, 45° turn bus in open space) to tune spacing maintenance. Then test with obstacles (like route a bus between two existing traces). Use KiCad’s DRC to ensure no clearance violations after routing.
    

**Which Algorithms Fit PNS Best?** Based on the above, the **leader-follower incremental approach** is most compatible with KiCad’s PNS architecture. It aligns with how PNS handles interactive routing – stepwise growth of a trace with immediate clearance checking and obstacle pushing. More advanced algorithms like global multi-commodity routing or ILP-based optimization are *overkill* here and don’t integrate with an interactive workflow. The PNS router already embodies an efficient obstacle-avoidance algorithm for single traces (with *look-ahead* to avoid getting stuck in cul-de-sacs). We should reuse that: effectively, we’ll still use PNS’s single-net router for the leader trace’s pathfinding (it can even use its lookahead A\* to suggest a path to the target). The followers then just replicate that path. If the leader’s A\* search accounts for obstacles, it will naturally route around them in a way that presumably the whole group can follow. If the bus is too wide for a gap that the leader (being just one trace) slipped through, our follower placement will detect a collision and then we’ll have to handle that (likely push others aside or choose a different route). This suggests maybe we should inflate obstacles for the leader’s pathfinding by the bus half-width – i.e., make the leader treat every obstacle as a bit larger. This can be done by temporarily increasing the clearance rule for the leader net to the bus spacing. For example, if normal clearance is 0.2mm but you have 8 traces with 0.2mm gap, effectively the leader should avoid obstacles by at least (bus\_width - trace\_width) to allow room for the rest. We can approximate that. This way, the leader’s auto path won’t go through an opening that followers can’t fit. This technique is akin to using a “shadow obstacle” or “clearance halo.” It simplifies follower placement because if the leader made it, likely everyone can. Implementation-wise, one could clone the board’s obstacle map and expand each obstacle by (N-1)\*clearance in the relevant direction for the leader’s search. Or hack: set leader’s clearance to bus\_width for pathfinding, then restore it when actually laying segments (because physically the leader trace is normal width).

-   **Trade-offs and Future Enhancements:** The straightforward implementation might not handle net reordering or automatic tuning – those can be left for future. The immediate benefit of having multi-track routing in KiCad will be huge for users, even if they sometimes have to manually adjust one trace that got stuck. Over time, enhancements could include: allow pausing the multi-route, dropping one net out, and continuing with the rest (for partial failure); integration with length matching (perhaps auto invoking the tuning tool after routing a bus net class); and support for bus shielding (maybe if a net class is tagged as shield, the router could automatically include it at edges or intervals).

**Integration with KiCad Files:** The user specifically mentioned KiCad files like `pns_line_placer.cpp` and `pns_multi_dragger.cpp`. To connect our plan to those:

-   In `pns_line_placer.cpp`, the core single-track router functions reside. We will likely *invoke* some of these for the leader net repeatedly. For instance, `LINE_PLACER::ProbeSegmentTowards(point)` might give a tentative segment (with 45° or straight) for the leader. We then validate and adjust it for followers.
    
-   In `pns_multi_dragger.cpp`, as analyzed, there are utilities for maintaining group structure. **Memory and Data Structures:** Using multiple `LINE` objects (one per net) to store partial routes in progress is fine. PNS uses a search graph for push-and-shove (to find path around obstacles). That will be invoked multiple times but each invocation is local, so memory impact is negligible. The number of additional objects is linear in nets count.

**Example Scenario:** Imagine implementing and testing on a DDR address bus (say 8 nets). The user selects the 8 pins on the RAM and the 8 pins on the controller (KiCad net classes can group them). They press the multi-route hotkey. The PNS router enters bus mode, highlighting those 8 ratsnest lines as a group. As the user moves the mouse, 8 trial traces extend from the pins, staying parallel. They navigate between other components; the router automatically pushes aside a bypass capacitor trace that was slightly in the way of one of the outer nets, moving it enough so the whole bundle passes (this happens because when that outer net’s segment collides, we call shove which moves the offending trace by the necessary distance – effectively chain-reacting to move it out of all 8’s path). The user clicks to fix segments or changes layers and sees 8 vias drop in a row. Finally, all 8 reach the destination pads and connect. The result: 8 neatly parallel routed tracks, all in one interactive operation. The user might then use KiCad’s built-in “Tune Net Length” on the net class to add small meanders so that all 8 have exactly equal length (if needed). Otherwise, they’re done – significantly faster than routing each net individually and manually maintaining spacing.

In conclusion, implementing simultaneous multi-track routing in KiCad is certainly feasible. By reusing PNS’s push-and-shove capabilities for each trace and coordinating them via a leader-follower strategy, we can achieve an experience comparable to Altium’s and others. The key coding tasks involve managing the relative positioning (the `offset` logic)[docs.kicad.org](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/structPNS_1_1MULTI__DRAGGER_1_1MDRAG__LINE.html#:~:text=bool%C2%A0dragOK%20%3D%20false), invoking collision/push checks in a loop for multiple nets, and ensuring user interactions (start, cornering, layer switch, finish, undo) are all handled consistently for the group. The effort is non-trivial but aligns well with KiCad’s modular router design. This feature, once added, would greatly enhance KiCad’s productivity for high-pin-count designs and is a logical next step given that differential pair support and multi-drag are already in place.
